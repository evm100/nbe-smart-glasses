Rebuilding the eye tracker

The current tracker is a line-by-line port of the lab's MATLAB V7 program, and it misses blinks. Now that the camera pipeline works and the picture is usable, we are starting the eye analysis over from first principles. This page explains what we considered and what we plan to build.

Draft plan25 Nov 2026Camera: OV2640 on XIAO ESP32-S3, 160 px crop, ~22 fps
The plan in short

Light the eye with infrared and raise the frame rate. Collect a set of controlled recordings with cued blinks and label them. From each frame, compute one number: how open the eye is, from 0 to 1. Compute it two ways and keep the better one: a quick baseline that needs no training, and a small trained network. Turn that signal into blink events, then into fatigue features such as blink duration and PERCLOS. Keep the network small enough that it can later run on the glasses.

  • IR, not visible lightAn 850 or 940 nm LED makes the pupil and lid edge clear, and the picture no longer depends on room lighting.
  • One signal: eye opennessBlinks, blink duration, partial blinks and PERCLOS all come from the same 0–1 trace.
  • Learned, not hand-tunedThe MATLAB tracker uses about 25 thresholds tuned by hand. The new one is trained on our own labeled frames.
  • Measured, not eyeballedWe judge every version against labeled, cued-blink recordings from wearers it never saw during training.

Why start over

The port finds the eye as the "darkest round blob" and calls the eye closed when that blob fades. When the lid comes down, eyelashes and the lid crease also form dark, roundish blobs, so a closed eye can still look half-open to the tracker. Its brightness normalisation and its "open-eye reference" both adapt over a few seconds, which partly cancels the change it is trying to detect. Its second blink detector, frame-to-frame motion, cannot tell a lid sweep from an eye movement or a head turn. The constants were tuned on a different camera at 80 px.

Patching it would mean tuning more thresholds by eye on a few hours of footage. The approach below replaces the guesswork with labels and measurement.

What a blink looks like to our camera

A blink lasts roughly 100–400 ms. The lid comes down fast and goes back up more slowly. At our current ~22 fps, the camera catches the closing phase in only about two frames.

The options

Eye-state methods fall into a few families. Methods built for a camera that sees the whole face don't transfer to a camera a few centimetres from one eye, so several popular ones are ruled out for us.

MethodHow it worksFor our near-eye IR cameraRole
Eye aspect ratioSoukupová & Čech Find face landmarks, then use the ratio of lid gap to eye width. Needs a full face in view. It's the common blog-post answer, and it doesn't apply here. ruled out
Pupil fittingStarburst, ElSe, PuRe Find the pupil edge and fit an ellipse. A blink shows up as "pupil lost". Strong for gaze under IR. For blinks it's weak: "pupil lost" has many causes. for gaze, later
Lid motionframe differences, optical flow Look for a burst of downward, then upward, movement in the lid region. Cheap and good at counting blinks. Confused by head motion; poor for durations. baseline
Eigen-eyesPCA on the session's own frames Learn the main ways the eye image varies. The strongest one usually tracks the lid. No labels needed, and it adapts to each wearer and fit. The quickest useful signal. baseline
Openness networksmall CNN, 64×64 input A trained network maps each frame to an openness value from 0 to 1. The current standard. Robust to lashes, make-up and fit, if the training data covers them. Small enough for a microcontroller. primary
Eye segmentationRITnet, EllSeg, OpenEDS Label every pixel as pupil, iris, sclera or lid. Openness and gaze both follow. Best single model for blinks and gaze, but much more labeling work. upgrade if gaze
Temporal modelhysteresis + timing rules → HMM / 1-D CNN Turns the per-frame trace into events: onset, closed, reopening, complete or partial. Needed by every option above. Timing is what separates a blink from looking down: a downward glance doesn't spring back. always

What we'll build

A layered pipeline: the input is fixed first, every frame becomes one openness number, and everything downstream is computed from that trace. Labels from controlled recordings feed both training and scoring.

About gaze velocity

The original idea was that gaze velocity tracks wellbeing. The literature supports that: saccadic peak velocity does drop with fatigue. But a saccade lasts only 20–100 ms, about one to two frames at 22 fps, so its velocity can't be measured with this camera. Eye trackers that report it sample at hundreds of Hz. The "gaze shift speed" in the current tracker is really "how far the eye moved between two frames". We suggest putting gaze on hold and leading with lid features, which our camera can measure, and revisiting gaze later if we add segmentation.

The data we need

A model is only as good as its labels. The few hours already recorded are useful for the baseline and for pretraining, but they come from few wearers and have no ground truth. A short, scripted session gives us both: each blink is cued by a beep whose time is logged, so we know roughly when blinks happened before anyone labels a frame.

Cued-blink session, about 10 minutes

  1. 60 sSit and look ahead, blink naturally blinksSpontaneous blinks, the kind we'll see in real use.
  2. 20 × 3 sBlink once at each beep blinksKnown times: ground truth before labeling.
  3. 10 × 4 sLong blink: hold closed for ~1 s longThe slow, long blinks that fatigue produces.
  4. 10 × 3 sHalf blink at each beep partialTeaches the in-between openness levels.
  5. 90 sFollow targets: down, up, left, right and back not a blinkLooking down lowers the lid, the main blink look-alike.
  6. 30 sSquint, smile, frown not a blinkThe lid moves for reasons other than blinking.
  7. 60 sTalk and turn the head not a blinkMotion and glasses shifting on the face.
  8. 90 sTake the glasses off, put them back, repeat step 2 blinksThe eye won't sit in the same place every session.

Run it twice per wearer under different room lighting. Aim for 8–10 wearers, with a mix of eye shapes, lash lengths and make-up.

Labeling: five openness levels per frame

The labeler scrubs through frames and presses a number key. Only frames near a blink need care, because long open stretches can be labeled in bulk. Labels are pre-filled from the cue times and the baseline, so most of the work is checking and correcting.

How we'll know it works

Every version of the tracker, including the current port, gets the same scorecard on held-out wearers. Each test wearer's recordings are kept out of training entirely, so the scores describe a new person putting the glasses on.

≤ 1 / min

The targets are proposals for us to agree on. Scoring the current port first tells us how far there is to go.

Roadmap

The phases are in order. Each phase ends with something we can measure, not just a finished piece of code.

  1. Phase 0

    Fix the input

    Fit the IR LED. 850 nm is easier for the sensor to see but glows faintly red; 940 nm is invisible but dimmer to the camera. Check whether the camera lens has an IR-cut filter: if it does, the LED won't help until the lens is swapped. Keep the LED at a low, measured power, well inside the eye-safety limits for IR (IEC 62471). Keep exposure fixed. Push the frame rate up using a smaller sensor window, aiming for 50 fps and accepting 30. Count dropped frames on Wi-Fi versus USB.

    Done when a recording shows a sharp pupil and lid edge at ≥ 30 fps with under 1 % of frames dropped.

  2. Phase 1

    Collect and label

    Add the cued session to the trial page (beeps with logged times). Build the labeling tool on the session page: a frame strip, an openness trace, and number keys. Record the first wearers.

    Done when 3 wearers are recorded and labeled, and the current port has a scorecard.

  3. Phase 2

    Baseline and event layer

    Build the PCA and lid-motion openness signal, turn it into blink events with hysteresis and timing rules, and compute per-minute features. This replaces the port on the live page.

    Done when the baseline beats the port on the scorecard.

  4. Phase 3

    The openness network

    Train a small CNN on 64×64 eye crops: start from public IR eye data (TEyeD, MRL Eye), then train on ours. Score it with leave-one-wearer-out, then quantise it to 8-bit and check the scores hold.

    Done when the network meets the targets above on unseen wearers.

  5. Phase 4

    On the microcontroller

    Run the 8-bit model on the ESP32-S3 with ESP-DL or TFLite Micro, so the glasses send openness values instead of raw frames.

    Done when the on-device output matches the server's output frame for frame.

  6. Phase 5

    Fatigue study

    Record sessions before and after a fatiguing task, and relate the lid features to self-reported sleepiness (e.g. the Karolinska scale) alongside PPG.

    Done when at least one lid feature separates rested from fatigued sessions within the same wearer.

Will it fit on the glasses?

Very likely, with room to spare. These are planning estimates for a network of about 50 thousand parameters; Phase 4 measures the real numbers.

The S3 has vector instructions that ESP-DL uses for 8-bit networks, so a model this size should take a few milliseconds per frame, well inside a 20 ms frame budget at 50 fps. If a later segmentation model is too big, the board to look at next is one with a small neural accelerator. We don't need to decide that now.

Open decisions

  • 850 or 940 nm LED: depends on whether the faint red glow bothers wearers, and on how bright the image is at a safe power.
  • Is gaze in or out for now? We suggest out: lid features first, gaze when segmentation is on the table.
  • Who labels, and how much? About two hours per wearer session; split across the team, it's a few afternoons.
  • Fatigue protocol for Phase 5: which fatiguing task, and what reference measure the professor wants to compare against.
